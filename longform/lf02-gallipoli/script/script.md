How they work together

   B-roll makes viewers feel it, the map makes them understand it, and the images
   make them believe it.

   Most images live on the map. They’re pinned to the exact place they happened, as
   photo cards, picture-in-picture panels or textures. So they add interest to the map
   instead of taking time away from it or from the B-roll.

   A few images go full-screen, at moments where a real photo hits harder than a
   reconstruction: the sinking battleship, the real drip rifle, the first Anzac Day, the
   memorial.

   Never show the same subject twice in a row as AI and as a real photo. Where a real
   photo exists for a key moment, the B-roll covers the feeling around it, not the identical
   scene.

   The rhythm: a typical 20-second stretch goes B-roll (feel), then map with an image
   card (understand plus proof), then back to B-roll or a full-screen photo (impact).


PART 2: Layer rules

MAP
   The series parchment terrain map, with the “IF AUSTRALIA” badge in the corner and
   pin-drop sounds.

   Blue: Allied. Red: Ottoman and Central Powers. Gold dotted lines: “what if” (alternate
   history), always.

   Map-to-B-roll uses the zoom-through: zoom into the pin, dissolve into the clip, then pull
   back out to the map.


B-ROLL
Style anchor (add to every prompt):
   Cinematic 1915 WWI documentary reconstruction, 16:9, 1080p or higher, muted sepia-
   tinted desaturated palette, subtle 35mm film grain, natural light, shallow depth of field,
   slow camera movement, realistic, no text, no captions, no logos, no modern objects.

For the present-day clips (B27, B30, B31, B32), swap the palette to “warm natural colour
palette, present day”.

Avoid list (add to every prompt):

   gore, blood, wounds, dead bodies, identifiable real people, caricatured faces, flags as the
   main subject, modern vehicles, text, watermarks.

Generate at 8–10 seconds and trim. When uploading, tick YouTube’s “altered or synthetic
content” option, and add a “Dramatised reconstruction” label on the first clip.


IMAGES
Four treatments, used throughout:

1. MAP CARD: a photo with a thin white border and drop shadow, pinned to its location on
   the map, with a slight 3D tilt. It stays on screen as the map moves beneath it.

2. PIP (picture-in-picture): a photo slides in from the screen edge while the map keeps
   animating. Good for showing “both sides” or a detail.

3. FULL-SCREEN KEN BURNS: a slow zoom or pan across the photo for 3–5 seconds,
   used only for high-impact moments.

4. PHOTO-TO-MAP MORPH: a full-screen photo shrinks into its pin on the map (or the
   map zooms into a pin and becomes the photo). This connects the real place with the
   map.

Image style rules

   Grade every photo to match the series: warm sepia, a little grain, and a soft vignette.
   Don’t colourise.

   Add a small caption on every image (place, date) in the map label font, and a tiny credit
   line in the corner (e.g. “Australian War Memorial”).

   Use slight parallax (cut the foreground from the background) for 2.5D movement on the
   full-screen photos. Never stretch or crop faces awkwardly.

   Real named people appear only as real photos (Churchill, Mustafa Kemal), never as
   AI.


Licensing
   Many Australian War Memorial photos from 1915 are out of copyright and marked for
   free use. Check the licence shown on each AWM collection page.

   Imperial War Museums, the US Library of Congress, Wikimedia Commons and Trove
   (National Library of Australia) also hold public-domain material. Check the licence on
   each item before use.

   Modern photos (the memorial, cemeteries, the dawn service) are usually copyrighted.
   Use Wikimedia Commons images with a compatible licence and credit them, or licensed
   stock.

PART 4: Paste-ready Grok voiceover (unchanged from v1)
(voice: Atlas)
Generate each chunk separately. Paste only the text under each label.

V01 In nineteen fifteen, Australia fought its first great battle as a nation… [pause] <slow>and
lost.</slow>

V02 Gallipoli. [pause] Eight months. More than eight thousand seven hundred Australians
dead. [pause] And yet, it became the most famous story Australia has.

V03 But what if they had won? [pause] What if the Anzacs had broken through, and the plan
had worked?

V04 Because the plan was huge. Win here, and the Allies could knock an entire empire out
of the war, reach Russia, and maybe end the First World War years early. [pause] Today, we
play it out, using real plans, real maps, and what historians actually think. [pause] And stay
for the twist at the end… because winning might have cost Australia something it can’t
imagine living without.

V05 First, the map. [pause] It’s nineteen fifteen, and the First World War is a nightmare. In
France, the armies are stuck in trenches that stretch from the sea all the way to Switzerland.

V06 Britain’s First Lord of the Admiralty, a young Winston Churchill, backs a bold idea.
[pause] Stop banging on the front door. Go around the back.

V07 The back door is here. The Dardanelles. A narrow strait in the Ottoman Empire, today’s
Türkiye. [pause] Push warships through it, and you reach Constantinople, today’s Istanbul,
the Ottoman capital.

V08 Take that, and three things happen at once. [pause] The Ottoman Empire, Germany’s
ally, could collapse. [pause] The sea route to Russia opens, so Britain and France can ship
weapons to their struggling ally. [pause] And Russia’s wheat can flow out to the world.

V09 On paper, it’s brilliant. [pause] On the eighteenth of March, nineteen fifteen, a huge
Allied fleet sails into the strait.
V10 But ten days earlier, at night, a small Ottoman minelayer called the Nusret had slipped
out and laid a line of about twenty mines… [pause] exactly where the big ships would turn.

V11 In a few hours, three battleships are sunk. Three more are badly damaged. [pause] The
fleet pulls back. [pause] And it never tries again.

V12 Here’s where the first what if hides. [pause] Some people, including Churchill, believed
the Ottoman forts were almost out of ammunition, and that one more push would have
broken through. [pause] Historians still argue about it. [pause] But the admirals didn’t push.
So the job went to the army. [pause] Including the Australians.

V13 The twenty-fifth of April, nineteen fifteen. [pause] Before dawn, in rowing boats, about
sixteen thousand Australians and New Zealanders head for the beach.

V14 They land at a narrow cove, under steep, scrubby cliffs. [pause] Whether it was the

Fact notes
   Casualties: about 8,700 Australians (DVA: 8,709) and about 2,700–2,800 New
   Zealanders were killed at Gallipoli. Ottoman deaths are usually given as 56,000 or more
   (estimates vary widely); British deaths were about 34,000 and French about 10,000.
   Over 50,000 Australians served. Sources: Department of Veterans’ Affairs; Australian
   War Memorial; Anzac Portal.

   The landing: about 16,000 Australians and New Zealanders landed on 25 April 1915 at
   Arı Burnu (Anzac Cove). Whether it was the intended landing point is debated, so the
   script says “historians still argue”.

   The 18 March 1915 naval attack: of the Allied battleships that entered the strait, three
   were sunk by mines (HMS Irresistible, HMS Ocean and the French Bouvet) and three
   badly damaged. The minelayer Nusret laid the final line of about 20–26 mines (sources
   vary) in Erenköy Bay on 8 March. Whether the forts were nearly out of ammunition is
   genuinely debated; Churchill believed so. Sources: Anzac Portal; Naval Historical Society
   of Australia; History.com.

   Mustafa Kemal: commanded Ottoman forces on the heights on 25 April. The “I don’t
   order you to attack, I order you to die” line comes from his later recollection, so the
   script paraphrases it as “he later recalled”. He founded the Republic of Turkey in 1923.

   August offensive: Lone Pine, the Nek and Chunuk Bair (6–10 August 1915). The
   Wellington Battalion held the summit of Chunuk Bair on 8 August before being pushed
  off.

  AE2: the Australian submarine AE2 (Lt Cdr Henry Stoker) was the first Allied submarine
  to get through the Dardanelles, on 25 April 1915. News of it reached commanders on the
  first night while withdrawal was being considered, and it is often cited as one factor in
  the decision to hold on (“said to be”, hedged). On 30 April it was damaged by the
  Ottoman torpedo boat Sultanhisar, and the crew scuttled it and were taken prisoner.
  Source: Australian War Memorial; Royal Australian Navy history.

  Evacuation: Anzac and Suvla were evacuated on 19–20 December 1915 with very few
  casualties, using deception including self-firing “drip rifles” (credited to William Scurry).
  Helles was evacuated on 9 January 1916.

  Counterfactual claims (Ottoman surrender, Bulgaria, Russia, Middle East borders) are
  presented as historians’ debate, not fact. Bulgaria joined the Central Powers in October
  1915.

  Anzac Cove: the Turkish government officially recognised the name in 1985. The
  memorial inscription at Arı Burnu is widely attributed to Atatürk, but its authorship is
  questioned by some historians, which is why the script says “attributed” and “historians
  debate”. It’s paraphrased, not quoted.

  Australia’s age: federation was in 1901, making it 14 years old in 1915.


Sensitivity notes
  Anzac Day and Gallipoli are deeply important to Australians and New Zealanders, and
  the campaign is a sacred national memory in Türkiye. There’s no humour anywhere in
  this episode.

  Present Ottoman soldiers with dignity, as defenders of their homeland. Use “Ottoman”
  for 1915 and “Türkiye/Turkey” for today. No caricatures, and no flags as the main
  subject.

  No gore. Death is shown only through counters, graves and silence. Don’t use any real
  photo showing the dead or wounded.

  Never put a real photo on an imagined (gold “what if”) event. Real images are only for
  things that actually happened.

  Every alternate-history outcome uses gold dotted lines on the map and hedged
  language (“might”, “some historians”), so speculation is never presented as fact.

  Avoid wider 1915 Ottoman politics (beyond this episode’s scope) and modern Turkish or
  Australian politics.
