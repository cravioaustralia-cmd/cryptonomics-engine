What If Japan Had Invaded Australia in 1942?
What changed from v1

   Map animation is the video. Around 80% of the runtime is map motion graphics.
   Moments that were B-roll in v1 (typewriter, treaty signing, troopship, trucks, the stamp)
   are now animated on the map.

   Only 12 B-roll moments, 3–5 seconds each, used where viewers need to feel the
   scene. Each one enters from the map and returns to it (see “Zoom-through” below).

   The voiceover is formatted for Grok text-to-speech, with paste-ready chunks in Part

PART 1: How it all fits together

The map is the main thread
   One continuous parchment-style terrain map, in the same style as your Shorts. The
   camera flies, zooms and pans across it the whole time.

   All information is shown as motion graphics on the map: arrows, routes, counters, unit
   icons, date labels, document and newspaper icons, and archive photos pinned as
   framed cards.

   Colours: red for Imperial Japanese movements, blue for Allied, and a desaturated map in
   grim moments. The palette turns warm in Act 5.

   Keep the “IF AUSTRALIA” badge in the corner throughout.


Zoom-through (how every B-roll enters and exits)
1. The map camera zooms fast into the exact pin where the moment happens.
2. The pin flares and dissolves into the B-roll clip (about 0.3 seconds).

3. The B-roll plays for 3–5 seconds.

4. The clip shrinks back into the pin and the camera pulls out to the map.

This makes B-roll feel like “stepping into” the map, so viewers never lose the thread.


Edit gaps vs. voice pauses
   Voice pauses ( [pause] , [long-pause] ) are inside the Grok text. They create natural
   breathing and dramatic beats within a chunk.

   Edit gaps (marked        in Part 2) are silence you leave between chunks in the video edit.
   That’s where B-roll, map reveals, music swells and sound effects play without narration.
   Grok can’t make exact timed pauses, so precise timing always happens in the edit.


Grok voiceover rules
   Tags used: [pause] (a short natural pause), [long-pause] (a longer pause for big
   moments), and <slow>…</slow> and <soft>…</soft> for a few key lines. These are
   Grok’s documented speech tags; use them sparingly.

   Delivery cues are not in the Grok text. Grok would read them aloud. The “Should
   sound” notes in Part 2 are for you to check each take; if a chunk sounds flat, regenerate
   it.

   Numbers and years are written as words in the Grok text (“nineteen forty-two”,
   “fifteen thousand”) so the voice never misreads them. “ANZUS” is written as “Anzus” so
   it’s said as a word, not letter by letter.

   Generate one chunk at a time (V01, V02…) and save each as its own audio file. This
   makes retiming to the picture easy, and keeps every request well under Grok’s character
   limit.

   Use the same voice (Atlas) and settings for every chunk so the narration stays
   consistent. If your tool has a speed setting, start slightly below normal speed;
   documentary narration sounds better a touch slower.

   Check these names by ear and respell them phonetically if Grok gets them wrong:
   Ulungura, Birdum, Kokoda, Milne Bay, Moresby.


B-roll rules (all 12 clips)
Add this style anchor to every prompt:

   Cinematic WWII-era documentary reconstruction, 16:9, 1080p or higher, muted
  desaturated colour palette, subtle 35mm film grain, natural light, shallow depth of field,
  slow camera movement, realistic, no text, no logos, no modern objects.

Add this “avoid” list to every prompt:

  gore, blood, visible wounds, dead bodies, identifiable real people, caricatured faces,
  rising sun flag, modern vehicles, modern clothing, text, watermarks.

   Generate at 5–8 seconds and trim to length.

   For B12 (the migrant ship), swap “muted desaturated” for “warm natural colour palette”.

   When uploading, tick YouTube’s “altered or synthetic content” option, and show a small
   “Dramatised reconstruction” label on the first B-roll.

   Real people appear only as archive photos on the map (John Curtin). Matthias Ulungura
   is shown only as a map label; use his photo only with permission and following Tiwi
   cultural protocols.
